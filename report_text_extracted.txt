Abstract
Illegal sand mining is one of India’s most pervasive and violent forms of organized
crime, yet it remains systematically under-studied and chronically under-documented.
This report presents a multi-lens data science investigation that confronts, as its defin-
ing challenge, the near-complete absence of cohesive, centralized data on illegal extrac-
tion activity in India. No single authoritative database exists. Instead, evidence of the
Sand Mafia is scattered across citizen field reports, journalistic archives, National Green
Tribunal court filings, government district surveys, environmental sensor streams, and
administrative housing records that were never designed to interoperate. Our principal
accomplishment is the synthesis of seven heterogeneous data sources spanning crim-
inal justice, housing policy, environmental monitoring, socioeconomic indicators, and
real-time sensor telemetry into a unified analytical framework: the India Sand Watch
platform, the National Data and Analytics Platform (NDAP), the Water Resources
Information System (WRIS), the Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB), Pradhan
Mantri Awas Yojana-Urban (PMAY-U) administrative records, Census 2011 socioe-
conomic tables, and two continuous water-quality sensors at Prayagraj. Drawing on
2,557 mining records, 91 court documents, 124 news reports, and 375 field observations
spanning 2001 to 2026, we apply exploratory spatial analysis, Difference-in-Differences,
Two-Stage Least Squares, synthetic control, bootstrapped mediation, NLP-based clas-
sification, and machine learning to construct, piece by piece, a picture of what illegal
sand mining looks like in the absence of direct observational data. We identify two
dominant mining corridors (the Gangetic plains and the Central India plateau), es-
tablish through causal inference methods that the 2015 PMAY-U launch caused a

1 Problem Statement Formulation
1.1 High-Level Thematic Problem
Sand is the second-most exploited natural resource in the world after water. It is the primary
input for concrete, glass, asphalt, and electronics manufacturing, making it indispensable to
modern construction and industry. The United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP)
estimates that more than 50 billion tonnes of sand and gravel are extracted globally every
year, a figure that dwarfs the replenishment capacity of rivers and coastal systems [1]. De-
mand is only expected to intensify: the United Nations estimates that by 2030, the world will
have 40 megacities (cities with populations exceeding 10 million), compared to 31 today [2],
each requiring enormous quantities of construction-grade aggregates. India sits at the center
of this pressure: it is one of the fastest-urbanizing nations on earth, with a government-
mandated housing program (the Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana-Urban, or PMAY-U) that
has sanctioned nearly 12 million new houses since its launch in 2015.
Because legally permitted sand extraction struggles to meet this accelerating demand, a
shadow supply chain has emerged. India’s “Sand Mafia” is an informal network of orga-
nized criminal groups that extract, transport, and sell sand illegally from rivers, floodplains,
and coastal areas. Their operations are frequently accompanied by violence: between 2019
and 2023, documented incidents include the deaths of government officials, journalists, and
civilians who attempted to report or obstruct mining activities [3]. Despite the scale and
severity of this phenomenon, academic and policy research on India’s Sand Mafia remains
sparse. Most existing literature treats it as a localized governance failure rather than a sys-
temic, nationally distributed organized crime problem with measurable structural drivers.
This report attempts to partially fill that gap. We treat illegal sand mining not as an isolated
criminal event but as a complex socioeconomic outcome shaped by construction demand,
poverty, enforcement capacity, environmental conditions, and geographic opportunity. Our
analysis proceeds through four thematic lenses:
1.Geographic lens: Where is illegal sand mining concentrated, and does it form spatial
clusters that suggest coordinated criminal networks rather than opportunistic individ-
ual actors?
2.Demand lens: Can we demonstrate a causal link between large-scale government
construction programs and the intensification of illegal extraction?
3.Socioeconomic lens: Are states with higher rates of poverty, marginal employment,
and poor housing conditions more prone to sustained illegal mining activity?
4.Environmental lens: Does proximity to active sand mining sites correlate with mea-
surable degradation in groundwater quality and air particulate levels?

1.2 Why Sand Mining Is Harmful
Before proceeding to the data, it is worth grounding the problem in its environmental and hu-
man consequences, because the urgency of these consequences is what justifies the analytical
effort.
Ecological destruction. Rivers are not static containers of water. They are dynamic sys-
tems in which sand and gravel serve as habitat for thousands of species, regulate streamflow,
anchor riverbanks, and filter groundwater. When sand is removed from riverbeds faster than
natural deposition can replace it (a process called riverbed incision ), channels deepen and
banks become unstable. This triggers a cascade: bridges lose their foundations, floodplains
no longer receive seasonal sediment, and the water table drops because the natural sponge
layer beneath the river is removed. Studies of heavily mined rivers in India have documented
bank collapses, loss of aquatic biodiversity, and the drying up of small tributaries [4].
Groundwater contamination. Sand mining disturbs the mineral-rich sediment layer of
riverbeds, releasing dissolved solids (including heavy metals) into the water column. Total
Dissolved Solids (TDS), the measure of the combined content of all inorganic and organic
substances dissolved in water, are a standard proxy for this contamination. Our analysis
of Water Resources Information System (WRIS) groundwater quality data across India,
examined in Section 2.2.1, finds that stations closer to active mining sites tend to show
elevated TDS readings, with the effect most pronounced in Bihar and West Bengal.
Air quality degradation. The transport of sand by truck, tractor, and barge generates
large quantities of coarse particulate matter. PM10 and PM2.5, the two standard regulatory
metrics for airborne particles (referring to particles with diameters of 10 and 2.5 micrometers
or less, respectively), have been linked to respiratory disease, cardiovascular damage, and
premature death in exposed populations. Our spatial regression analysis finds that PM10 is
the most strongly correlated environmental variable with mining intensity at the state level.
Violence and impunity. The Sand Mafia operates in a context of systemic legal impunity.
Court documents in our dataset reveal patterns in which local officials are allegedly bribed,
whistleblowers are threatened, and environmental regulations are routinely violated without
sanction. The gap between observed mining intensity and the rate of court-recognized legal
cases (analyzed in Section 2.2.4) is itself a measure of this impunity.
Social inequality. Illegal sand mining is not uniformly distributed across society. It cor-

resource-dependent areas, where livelihoods are most vulnerable to environmental disrup-

This makes illegal sand mining both an environmental problem and a question of social
justice.
1.3 The Data Integration Challenge
The central methodological challenge of this project is one that no amount of analytical
sophistication can fully overcome: there is no single, authoritative, comprehensive dataset
on illegal sand mining in India. Unlike conventional crime statistics, which are collected
by a centralized agency (the NCRB) and published in a standardized annual format, sand
mining incidents are scattered across field observation networks, journalistic archives, court
filings, government district surveys, and environmental monitoring databases that were never
designed to interoperate.
This fragmentation is not accidental. The Sand Mafia thrives in informational darkness: the
absence of systematic documentation is itself a governance failure that perpetuates impunity.
When there is no official count of mining incidents, there is no official accountability for failing
to address them.
Our response to this challenge is the defining accomplishment of this project: the assembly
of a unified analytical framework from seven fundamentally different data sources.
1.India Sand Watch (Veditum India Foundation): 2,557 incident records from
field observers, journalists, court documents, and government district surveys, spanning
2001 to 2026. This is the only publicly available national-scale mining incident database
in India. Its coverage is uneven (Bihar and Madhya Pradesh are overrepresented;
northeastern states are nearly absent), and its records are heterogeneous in quality
and completeness.
2.National Data and Analytics Platform (NDAP): Official government statistical
series from the NCRB (crime and police personnel), Census 2011 (population, literacy,
marginal workers, household conditions), CPCB (air quality), and PMAY-U (housing
construction progress and central assistance allocations).
3.Water Resources Information System (WRIS): 7,190 groundwater monitor-
ing station records with location coordinates and Total Dissolved Solids (TDS) mea-
surements, spatially linked to the nearest confirmed mining point for Geographically
Weighted Regression.
4.Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB): State-level annual average PM2.5 and
PM10 concentrations from the national ambient air quality monitoring network.

The result is not a perfect dataset. It is the most complete picture of illegal sand mining
in India that can currently be assembled from publicly available sources, and demonstrating
that it is possible to derive meaningful systemic analysis from these fragments is a contribu-
tion to future research in this area.

1.5 Exploratory Data Analysis Results
1.5.1 Mining Observations
The 375 field observation records span 2016 to 2023, with the majority concentrated af-
ter 2017. Bihar is the single most-reported state with 193 observations, followed by Uttar
Pradesh (48), West Bengal (48), and Madhya Pradesh (43). This distribution is notably
skewed: Bihar alone accounts for 51% of all field observations. However, the broader unified
geo dataset (2,212 records) tells a different story at the state level: Madhya Pradesh leads
with 369 incidents, followed by Maharashtra (210), Uttar Pradesh (130), and Tamil Nadu
(125). The divergence between observation data and aggregated incident data reflects dif-
ferences in data collection methodology: field observers are concentrated in certain regions,
while the aggregated data incorporates news reports, court documents, and survey data that
are more uniformly distributed.
A key early finding is the severe mismatch between states in terms of “formal” versus “infor-
mal” reporting. Bihar has 193 informal field observations but very few corresponding court
cases. Madhya Pradesh, by contrast, has 279 news-level incidents and a substantial body
of NGT court filings. This asymmetry hints at differential legal enforcement, and motivates
the classification analysis in Section 2.2.4.

1.5.2 Court Documents
The 91 court documents span cases from the Supreme Court, various High Courts, and
the National Green Tribunal. Topic modeling (using Latent Dirichlet Allocation, a tech-

the Madhya Pradesh subset identified five dominant topic clusters: (1) illegal mining and

“illegal mining,” 26 referenced a failure to obtain environmental clearance, and 22 cited en-

engaged with environmental rather than criminal dimensions of the problem.

Mining incidents in the unified dataset show a dramatic and statistically significant increase
beginning in 2015, the year PMAY-U was launched. Pre-2015 incidents average 13 per year;
post-2015 incidents average 191 per year: a 14.7 ×increase. This temporal break is the
starting point for the causal analysis in Section 2.2.2.

2 Database System
2.1 Storing the Data
Given the diverse and multi-format nature of our data sources (CSV files from NDAP,
GeoJSON shapefiles, CSV exports from India Sand Watch, and raw sensor logs), we adopted
a hybrid storage architecture appropriate for a research-scale project.
The primary relational store is a PostgreSQL database hosted on Supabase, a cloud-hosted
open-source database platform. All mining observation, court document, news report, and
district survey data are stored in a unified table ( nomobs unified geodata ) with standard-
ized column schemas. Geographic data (state and district polygon shapefiles) are managed as
GeoJSON files read directly into Python via the GeoPandas library (an extension of the Pan-
das data analysis library that can handle geographic geometries). Environmental datasets
(air quality, groundwater quality, sensor data) are stored as flat CSV files and joined on
normalized state names at query time.
A key challenge throughout this project was state name normalization. Official government
datasets, India Sand Watch records, and geographic shapefiles use inconsistent spellings
and abbreviations for the same administrative unit (for example, “Andaman & Nicobar,”
“Andaman and Nicobar Islands,” and “Andaman Nicobar”). We implemented a systematic
normalization pipeline using Unicode normalization (to remove diacritics), lowercase conver-
sion, punctuation stripping, and a manually curated correction dictionary mapping known
variant spellings to a canonical form. This pipeline was applied uniformly across all datasets
before any merge operation.
For the spatial dashboard handoff (documented in dashboard handoff resource.ipynb ),
all analysis outputs were serialized as GeoJSON layer files and a JSON manifest, providing
us with a well-defined interface independent of the analysis codebase.

2.2.1 Spatial Distribution and Clustering (spatial analysis.ipynb)
Kernel Density Estimation. We first constructed a continuous spatial density surface
over India using Kernel Density Estimation (KDE). KDE is a non-parametric statistical
technique that estimates the probability density of a variable (in this case, mining incidents)
at each point in a geographic area, by placing a smooth “kernel” (a mathematically defined
bump) at each observed data point and summing the overlapping kernels. Unlike a simple

dot map, KDE produces a continuous surface that reveals where events are most densely
concentrated.
Using 373 geo-coded mining points from the unified dataset (the subset with valid latitude
and longitude coordinates), KDE with a bandwidth of 0.08 degrees identifies two distinct
concentration corridors:
1.The Gangetic Plains corridor: A high-density zone stretching along the Ganga
river and its tributaries through Bihar, eastern Uttar Pradesh, and West Bengal. In
Bihar alone, the Ganga river accounts for 142 of 268 total records, with the Patna
district contributing 105 records: the single hottest geographic concentration in the
country.
2.The Central India Plateau corridor: A high-density zone across Madhya Pradesh
and Rajasthan, driven primarily by mining on the Chambal and Narmada rivers.
Morena district in Madhya Pradesh has the highest district-level count nationally at 81
records, reflecting the Chambal’s relatively remote and politically sensitive geography.
We also constructed a “mining-to-monitoring ratio” map comparing mining incident density
against groundwater monitoring station density. This map reveals that northeastern districts
of India have high mining incident density but relatively low monitoring station coverage,
identifying probable under-detection zones where the true scale of illegal extraction likely
exceeds what the data capture.

surprising: geography (which rivers a state contains) and political economy (which networks

To test whether spatial dependence among states biases the OLS estimates, we computed
Lagrange Multiplier tests for spatial lag dependence and spatial error dependence. Neither
test reaches significance ( p= 0.98 for the lag test, p= 0.74 for the error test), so OLS is

Geographically Weighted Regression (GWR). Standard regression assumes that the
relationship between predictors and outcomes is the same everywhere. Geographically
Weighted Regression relaxes this assumption by allowing regression coefficients to vary across
space: at each location, a separate regression is estimated using a locally weighted subset of
nearby observations, where closer observations receive higher weights. This produces a map
of locally varying coefficients rather than a single global estimate.
We applied GWR to the relationship between groundwater quality (log-transformed Total

2.2.4 NLP-Based Classification of Illegality (classification.ipynb)
A core analytical challenge is that not every observed mining incident in the India Sand
Watch data is unambiguously illegal. Some observations may describe permitted operations

incorrectly identified as illegal; others may describe genuinely illegal mining without using
legal terminology. We designed a rule-based scoring system to classify field observations by
their probability of reflecting illegal activity, drawing on patterns derived from the court
document corpus.
Feature Engineering. For each of the 375 field observations, we extract three classes of
signals from the free-text “Notes from the location” field:
1.Operational signals: Binary indicators for whether the text mentions machinery (JCBs,
excavators, pumps) and transport vehicles (trucks, tractors, trolleys). These are
weighted most heavily (3 points each) because large-scale mechanized extraction is defi-
nitionally inconsistent with small-scale permitted mining under the Mines and Minerals
(Development and Regulation) Act.
2.Risk signals: Binary indicators for nighttime activity, high-volume extraction, and
repeated/daily operations (1 to 1.5 points each). These amplify the illegality score
when present alongside operational signals.
3.Legal signals: Pattern matching against nine regular expression (text pattern) rules
derived from legal language found in court documents: references to illegal mining,
unauthorized operations, lack of environmental clearance, lease violations, and excess
extraction (2 points each match).
Classification Thresholds. Observations scoring above the 80th percentile of the total
score distribution are classified as highly likely illegal ; those between the 50th and 80th
percentiles are classified as possibly illegal . Of 375 observations, 121 (32%) are classified as
highly likely illegal and 254 (68%) as possibly illegal.
Findings. The states with the highest absolute counts of highly likely illegal observations
are Uttar Pradesh (32), Madhya Pradesh (30), West Bengal (25), and Bihar (19). When
normalized by total observations per state, the highest proportions are in Goa (100%),
Himachal Pradesh (100%), Rajasthan (87.5%), and Madhya Pradesh (69.8%). Bihar has the
lowest proportion (9.8%) despite having the most total field observations, which is consistent
with its large volume of lower-intensity, possibly artisanal (small-scale, manual) extraction
that does not trigger the operational machinery signals.
Enforcement Gap Analysis. We compute, for each state, the gap between the rate

severely lagging behind documented criminal activity. Himachal Pradesh, West Bengal, and
Jharkhand show the largest positive gaps (more observed illegal activity than court activity),

indicating that the enforcement environment is at least as strong a determinant of
mining location as environmental conditions.
4 Recommendations and Policy Suggestions
Based on the empirical evidence developed in this analysis, we offer the following policy
recommendations, organized by institutional actor.
4.1 For the Central Government
Decouple PMAY-U sand demand from illegal sources. The causal evidence from
our DiD, IV, and synthetic control analyses converges on a finding that large-scale centrally-
sponsored housing construction generates demand that is being partially met by illegal ex-
traction. The central government should require state governments to source sand for PMAY
projects exclusively from verified legal stockpiles, and should establish a Sand Sourcing Com-
pliance Audit as a condition of central assistance release. This transforms a demand-side
driver of illegal mining into a demand-side enforcement lever.
Establish a national sand price floor and distribution system. A significant driver of
illegal sand’s attractiveness is its price: illegal sand sold without royalties and transportation
duties is substantially cheaper than legally extracted sand. A centrally-set price floor for

legally mined sand, combined with state-managed distribution depots (similar to the cement
price control boards that existed historically), would narrow the price differential that makes
illegal supply competitive.
Fund continuous environmental monitoring in high-mining corridors. The Ganga/Sangam
sensor analysis demonstrates the feasibility and value of continuous, automated water quality
monitoring. We recommend deploying continuous conductivity and TDS monitoring buoys
at 20 to 30 sites along the Ganga, Chambal, and Narmada rivers, with data publicly acces-
sible in real time. This would create an objective, legally admissible evidentiary record of
environmental damage linked to specific time periods and locations.
4.2 For State Governments
District-level police deployment in high-gap states. Our enforcement gap analysis
identifies Himachal Pradesh, West Bengal, Jharkhand, Gujarat, and Goa as states where
observed illegal mining greatly exceeds court-recognized cases. State governments in these
jurisdictions should deploy dedicated sand mining enforcement cells at the district level,
with specific performance metrics (cases filed per observed incident) tied to district collector
accountability.
Implement machine-verified transport permits. A large portion of illegal sand escapes
detection not at the extraction point but during transport. A GPS-tracked, QR-code-verified
e-permit system for sand transport vehicles (modeled on the e-way bill system used for
goods transport under GST) would allow enforcement agencies to intercept illegal shipments
at checkpoints without needing to monitor every river stretch. Several states, including
Rajasthan and Madhya Pradesh, have piloted versions of such systems; these should be
made mandatory and standardized.
Alternative livelihood programs for marginal workers. The correlation between
marginal worker ratios and mining intensity points to a labor supply dimension of the prob-
lem. Sand mining offers seasonal income to workers who lack stable alternatives. State
employment guarantee programs (under the Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment
Guarantee Act, MGNREGA) should be expanded and prioritized in high-mining districts
during the dry-season months (October through February) when mining intensity peaks.
This directly competes with illegal mining for the available labor pool.
4.3 For Judicial and Regulatory Bodies
Standardize NGT enforcement orders. Our court document analysis finds that NGT
orders are the most common form of legal response to illegal sand mining, but their en-
forcement is highly variable. The NGT should issue standardized compliance affidavit re-

Treat sand mining mafia cases under organized crime legislation. Currently, most
sand mining prosecutions proceed under the Mines and Minerals (Development and Reg-

The organized, cross-district, and often interstate nature of Sand Mafia operations (as ev-
idenced by the HH spatial clustering of Goa, Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh, and Rajasthan)
qualifies these networks for prosecution under the Maharashtra Control of Organised Crime
Act (MCOCA) and its state-level equivalents. This would enable asset forfeiture, longer
custody periods, and stronger deterrence.
4.4 For Civil Society and Research
Expand the India Sand Watch observer network. Our data quality analysis reveals
severe geographic gaps in field observation coverage: northeastern states, Odisha, Chhat-
tisgarh, and Kerala are almost entirely absent from the mining observation dataset despite
having significant rivers. India Sand Watch should invest in trained observer networks in
these states, particularly targeting riverine communities directly affected by extraction.
Longitudinal panel datasets for causal inference. The primary limitation of our causal
analyses is the cross-sectional nature of most available data. A district-level, annually up-
dated panel dataset combining mining incidents, police deployment, PMAY completion rates,
and environmental quality would allow much stronger causal identification using panel fixed
effects methods. We encourage NDAP and state data agencies to prioritize the assembly of
such a panel.
Limitations
We acknowledge several important limitations of this analysis. First, the mining observation
data suffer from reporting bias: regions with more active civil society organizations, better
internet connectivity, and more sympathetic local governance are more likely to have observa-
tions recorded. This means our spatial findings probably undercount mining in more remote
or politically sensitive areas. Second, the causal analyses rely on state-level aggregations with
small effective sample sizes (33 states for the cross-sectional OLS, and a limited number of
pre-treatment periods for the DiD event study). While we have implemented robustness
checks (sensitivity analysis excluding the three most data-heavy states, 2SLS to address en-
dogeneity), these limitations bound the strength of causal claims we can make. Third, the
GWR and water sensor analyses are observational: we cannot definitively attribute elevated
